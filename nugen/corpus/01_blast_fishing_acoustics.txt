Blast fishing acoustics: what an underwater explosion sounds like

Blast fishing, also called bomb fishing or dynamite fishing, is the practice of detonating an improvised explosive in the water to stun or kill fish so that they can be collected at the surface. The charges are often bottles filled with fertiliser-based explosive and a detonator. The practice is illegal in most countries and has been documented in dozens of them. It destroys coral structure, leaving fields of rubble where living reef once stood, and the damage can last for decades. For acoustic monitoring, blast fishing is unusually favourable because an underwater explosion is one of the loudest and most distinctive sounds a hydrophone can record.

An underwater explosion produces a shock wave followed by an oscillating gas bubble. On a recording this appears as a very sharp onset, typically rising from background level to peak in a few milliseconds. The energy is broadband, meaning it spreads across a wide range of frequencies, with a strong low-frequency component that carries far through water. After the first pulse, the bubble expands and collapses repeatedly, producing a series of weaker pulses at regular intervals. This bubble pulse tail is a useful signature because many other transient sounds do not have it. The main pulse is usually well under a second long, though reverberation from the seabed and surface can extend the audible tail for several seconds in shallow water.

Sound travels more than four times faster in water than in air and loses energy slowly, so a single recorder can hear blasts from very far away. Studies in Indonesia reported detection of explosions out to roughly seventeen kilometres from one low-cost recorder. Distance changes what the recording looks like. A near blast is extremely loud and sharp. A distant blast arrives smoother, with the high frequencies absorbed and the onset softened, which makes it harder to tell from other sounds. For this reason a detection system should always report an uncertainty about distance, and a single sensor gives only an area, not an exact point.

Several sounds can be mistaken for a blast. A misfiring boat engine can produce a sudden loud bang. Thunder and heavy rain create broadband noise at the surface. Snapping shrimp produce many sharp clicks, but these are individually weak, very short, and arrive in dense crackling bursts rather than one isolated slam. Seismic events and construction work such as pile driving also create strong low-frequency impulses. A good classifier uses several cues together: the onset speed, whether the event is isolated, the bubble pulse pattern, the spectral shape, and the time of day. Blast fishing in some regions peaks in the morning and drops on certain days of the week, so repeated events at a similar time are meaningful context.

Because false accusations have real consequences for fishing communities, an automated system should treat a detection as a lead, not a verdict. The recommended workflow is detect, classify, estimate location with an uncertainty, state the confidence honestly, and send the event to a human for review when confidence is moderate or the sound could be a look-alike. A ranger should be told what the system heard, why it matters, how sure it is, and what the sensible next step is. Evidence handling matters too: the audio clip, the time stamp, the sensor identity and the model version should be kept so that the detection can be checked later.
