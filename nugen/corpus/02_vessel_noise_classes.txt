Vessel noise classes and how they sound underwater

Ships and boats are the most common human sound source on a coastal hydrophone, and recognising them matters for two reasons. First, vessel noise masks the natural sounds of the reef and can stress marine animals. Second, correctly labelling vessels prevents them from being confused with a blast. This document describes the main vessel types a monitoring system will meet along a tropical coast and the acoustic cues that separate them.

Small outboard fishing boats are the most frequent source near reefs. Their engines produce a rhythmic low-frequency hum with strong harmonics, often between about fifty and a few hundred hertz, plus a broadband hiss from the propeller. When the boat accelerates the pitch rises and the level climbs over many seconds. A boat passing close can be very loud, but the level rises and falls smoothly as it approaches and leaves. This smooth envelope is the clearest difference from a blast, whose onset takes only a few milliseconds.

Inboard diesel fishing vessels and small cargo boats produce a deeper, steadier tone with a visible stack of harmonic lines on a spectrogram. Slow-turning propellers create a pulsing pattern called blade rate, a repeating amplitude modulation that is heard as a throb. Counting the throb rate gives a rough idea of engine speed. Larger ships radiate very strong low frequencies that travel for tens of kilometres, so a distant ship may appear as a faint, continuous band at the bottom of the spectrogram for an hour or more.

Cavitation occurs when a propeller turns fast enough to form small bubbles that collapse. It adds a bright, noisy hiss at high frequencies and is common in speedboats and when a vessel is working hard. Cavitation noise can look broadband, which is one reason a simple loudness threshold cannot separate vessels from other events. A classifier needs the temporal pattern as well as the spectrum.

Engine misfires and backfires are the main vessel-related false alarm. A sudden bang from an engine can have a fast onset, but it usually sits inside a steady engine tone, and it lacks the long low-frequency tail and bubble pulse pattern of an explosion. If a sharp event happens while a vessel tone is clearly present, the system should lower its confidence and ask for human review instead of declaring a blast.

Useful cues for a vessel decision include a smooth rise and fall of level, harmonic lines, blade-rate modulation, a gradual change in pitch with speed, and persistence for tens of seconds or minutes. Useful cues against a vessel decision include an isolated sharp onset, no harmonic structure, and a decaying tail with repeated weak pulses. When a vessel passes several sensors in sequence, the time delay between sensors also gives its speed and heading, which can help rangers identify patrol targets.

Rangers should treat vessel detections as context, not as violations. Most boats are legal fishing and transport. A vessel detection becomes interesting when it coincides with a blast event, when it appears inside a closed zone, or when it appears at times when no legal activity is expected. The briefing for a vessel event should state the likely type, how sure the system is, whether it is moving, and whether any other event happened nearby in time.
