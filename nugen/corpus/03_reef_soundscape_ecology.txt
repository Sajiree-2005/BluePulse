Reef soundscape ecology: why a healthy reef is noisy

A coral reef is not silent. A healthy reef is one of the noisiest habitats in the ocean, and much of that sound comes from living animals. Listening to this soundscape gives a way to monitor a reef continuously, without divers and without disturbing the animals. The sound that animals make is called biophony, the sound of waves and weather is called geophony, and the sound of human activity is called anthrophony. Reef monitoring compares these three over time.

Snapping shrimp are the dominant source on most tropical reefs. Each animal snaps a claw to create a cavitation bubble that collapses with a sharp click. Thousands of shrimp together create a dense crackling that sounds like frying oil, mostly in the band from about two to twenty kilohertz. Because the crackle is continuous, it forms a background level that changes with shrimp numbers and activity. Many studies treat a drop in this crackle as a possible sign of habitat decline, although the link depends on the site.

Fish add lower-pitched sounds, usually in the range of roughly one hundred to a thousand hertz. Many species grunt, pop, knock or hum. Chorusing is strongest at dusk and dawn, when fish gather to feed or spawn. A reef recorded across a full day shows a daily rhythm, with a rise in low-frequency energy around sunset and again near sunrise. Losing this rhythm is a warning sign worth investigating.

Acoustic indices summarise the soundscape with single numbers. The acoustic complexity index measures how much the sound changes over short times in each frequency band, so a busy reef with varied biological sound scores higher. Spectral entropy measures how evenly energy is spread across frequencies. Band energy ratios compare the low-frequency fish band with the high-frequency shrimp band. These indices are cheap to compute on a small device and can be sent to the cloud every minute.

An index only means something relative to its own site. Different reefs have different baselines because of depth, substrate, species and recorder placement. The sensible method is to record for a period, build a baseline for each hour of the day, and then measure how far new values sit from that baseline. A large and persistent deviation is a prompt to look closer, not a diagnosis. Weather, tides, moon phase and nearby boats also change the soundscape, so deviations should be read together with those factors.

Blast fishing and heavy boat traffic both degrade the soundscape. Repeated explosions break coral and kill the animals that make the sound, and the loss may show up weeks later as a lower baseline. Reef recovery can also be tracked acoustically, because returning fish and invertebrates raise biological sound again. A monitoring report should describe the trend, the size of the change from the baseline, how long it has lasted, and the limits of the evidence.

Recording practice affects every number above. A hydrophone placed in a sandy patch hears less shrimp crackle than one placed among coral heads, and a recorder moved by a few metres can change the baseline. For that reason each sensor keeps a fixed position, a fixed gain and a logged depth, and any change to the setup is written down so that a sudden shift in the data can be separated from a real change in the reef.
