Look-alike sounds and how to avoid false alarms

A blast detector that raises too many false alarms will soon be ignored, and one that misses real blasts defeats its purpose. Reducing false alarms without losing real events requires knowing what else can sound like an explosion and what features separate them. The field experience from reef monitoring programmes is that a human reviewer remains necessary, because some sounds are genuinely ambiguous.

The most common look-alike is a boat engine. An engine that misfires, backfires or suddenly changes speed can produce a loud, short bang. Outboard motors being started or revved near a recorder can also create sharp bursts. Unlike a blast, engine noise is usually accompanied by a running engine before and after the event, with tonal components at fixed frequencies. A blast typically arrives out of a quiet background and is followed by silence or a decaying tail, not by a continuing engine hum.

Snapping shrimp produce clicks, and occasionally a loud click or burst of clicks close to the hydrophone can resemble a small blast in a quick look. The difference is the pattern. Shrimp sound is a dense crackle made of many tiny clicks, whereas a blast is a single strong pulse, often with a bubble oscillation tail. Shrimp clicks are also concentrated at high frequencies, while a blast carries strong low-frequency energy.

Surface weather produces sudden broadband noise. Thunder, heavy rain squalls and breaking waves on a nearby reef can raise the background level rapidly. Rain tends to be a continuous hiss rather than an isolated pulse. Thunder may produce a strong low-frequency event but usually with a rolling, drawn-out character and often with lightning reports from the surface. Wind gusts over the sea add noise that varies smoothly.

Other impulsive sources include construction work such as pile driving and dredging, seismic survey airguns, and genuine earthquakes. Pile driving repeats at regular intervals, which is a clear giveaway. Airguns produce repeated pulses every ten seconds or so. Earthquakes produce low-frequency sound with a long rumbling character. Cables and chains knocking against the recorder, fishing gear striking the housing and animals touching the device can produce short noises that appear only on one sensor and are extremely close.

Practical rules help. First, look for an isolated onset with a very fast rise, within a few milliseconds. Second, check the spectrum for broadband energy including strong low frequencies. Third, look for the bubble pulse tail, a series of decaying pulses at regular spacing. Fourth, compare with other sensors: a real blast far away will often be heard on several sensors with consistent time delays, whereas a bump on one housing will not. Fifth, consider context: time of day, recent similar events and weather.

In BluePulse a detection is labelled uncertain rather than forced into a class when probabilities are close or the event does not match any class well. Uncertain events go to the review queue. A confirmed false alarm, marked by a reviewer, is valuable training data and should be saved with the audio clip so that the classifier can learn from the mistake. The goal is not zero false alarms but a manageable number, for example at most one per sensor per day, while keeping missed blasts as rare as possible.
