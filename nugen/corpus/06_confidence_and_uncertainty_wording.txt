Confidence and uncertainty: how BluePulse should word its findings

A monitoring system speaks to people who must decide whether to send a boat, wake a supervisor or simply note an event. The way a finding is worded influences those decisions, so wording is part of the safety design. BluePulse follows a simple rule: say what was heard, say how sure the system is, say what could be wrong, and say what a sensible next step is. It never states more certainty than the evidence supports and it never accuses a person.

There are three different kinds of confidence in the system, and they should not be mixed up. The first is the classifier probability, the number produced by the audio model for each class, such as blast ninety-one percent. This describes how well the sound matches what the model learned. The second is the language model confidence score, a number from zero to one hundred reported by the Nugen aligned model that shows how well the generated briefing stays within the domain knowledge it was aligned on. The third is the human reviewer decision, which confirms or rejects the event. A briefing should mention the classifier probability in plain words and may mention the confidence score as a measure of how well grounded the text is.

Wording follows bands. When the language confidence score is eighty or above and the classifier probability is high, the briefing may use direct language such as consistent with blast fishing and recommend dispatch. When the score is between fifty and seventy-nine, the briefing must say that the finding needs human review before action, and it should list the alternative explanations. When the score is below fifty or the classifier marks the event as uncertain, the briefing should say that the system cannot reliably interpret the sound, give the reason, and send the event to review. The exact thresholds are calibrated on test data and may change, so the system reads them from configuration rather than hard-coding them.

Good briefings use calibrated verbs. Heard, consistent with, resembles and likely are acceptable for sound evidence. Confirmed, caught, guilty and illegal fishing by a named boat are not acceptable, because sound alone cannot confirm those facts. Locations should be given as an area with a radius, for example about four kilometres south-west of the sensor, unless several synchronised sensors give a position estimate, in which case the estimate and its uncertainty should be stated.

A briefing should always include the key numbers a reviewer needs: the time in local time and in universal time, the sensor name, the class, the probability, the number of sensors that heard the event, and the sound level relative to background. It should be short enough to read on a phone, three or four sentences, and should put the recommended action first when the severity is high.

Uncertainty must be honest about what is not known. Examples of useful statements are a boat engine misfire cannot be excluded, the event was heard on only one sensor so the position is uncertain, the recording was clipped so the true level was higher, and the sensor clock was not synchronised. If the system does not have enough information, the correct answer is that it does not know and the briefing should say what extra information would help.

When a reviewer overrides the system, the briefing record is kept, and the correction becomes part of the audit trail. Disagreement between the classifier and the language model is itself informative. If the classifier says blast but the language model confidence is low, the event should be flagged for review rather than dispatched silently. The language model may add caution or context, but it must never remove an alert that the classifier raised.
